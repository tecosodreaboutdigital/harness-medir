Glossary

Every term underlined across parts 1 to 4 is defined once, here. Hover a term anywhere in the series for the short definition, or click it to arrive at this exact entry. References point to the origin of each concept, never to a second-hand summary.

Fernando Teco Sodré · Updated August 2026

agent: A system that combines a language model with tools, memory and rules to carry out multi-step tasks, deciding at each step what to do next. The working equation is agent equals model plus harness. Introduced in part 1.

agent office: The governance function that answers three questions no single agent's own controls can answer: how many agents exist, who owns each one, and which ones still pay for themselves. Not a product a company buys, a function it organises. Introduced in part 4.

agent owner: The role that operates an agent and answers for the risk it generates. A named person, never a department, and never combined with the certifier role. Introduced in part 4.

AGENTS.md: A file at the root of a project that tells the agent where things are, what the conventions are and what not to do. Good practice treats it as a short index, not a manual. Community specification. Source

andon: A visual or audible signal that shows process status and halts the line to call for immediate help when a problem appears. With agents, it is escalation to a human with the context attached. Toyota Production System. Source

append-only log: A record in which nothing can be altered or deleted, only added at the end. It is the technical basis of any trustworthy audit trail, and what makes an execution reconstructable, replayable and reversible.

area sponsor: The role that answers for the return an agent's briefing promised, checked at revalidation against the return it actually delivered. Introduced in part 4.

Ashby's Law: The law of requisite variety: a regulator can only control a system if it has at least as much variety as the system it governs. It explains why standardising task types makes agent control achievable. W. Ross Ashby, 1956. Source

authority limit: The boundary of what someone, or some system, can decide on their own. In a harness, it is defined by class of action and enforced outside the model. Introduced in part 2.

automation bias: The tendency of a human reviewer to trust or over-rely on a system's output without genuinely checking it. Named explicitly in article 14 of the EU AI Act as a failure the person in charge must remain aware of. EU AI Act, article 14. Source

autonomous delegation: A delegation pattern where the agent carries its own identity and permission set, independent of any human session. The pattern for background jobs and agent-to-agent communication, and the one that concerns regulators most. Introduced in part 3.

cadence: A fixed frequency at which a check runs, regardless of whether a task is in progress. It is what detects drift, which no single run reveals.

certifier: The role that approves an agent's autonomy tier and sets its certification's validity date. Cannot be held by the same person as the agent owner. Introduced in part 4.

concentration: The failure mode where one system proposes a plan, authorises its own risk and executes the effect, with nothing independent checking any of the three. Introduced in part 3.

context engineering: The discipline of deciding what enters the model's field of view on each run: which documents, rules, examples and prior results. It precedes the harness and keeps operating inside it. Birgitta Böckeler's formulation for coding agents. Source

context injection: Inserting text into the agent's reading field during execution. It is dangerous when it arrives from outside with no control, and it is the central technique of the sensor that teaches when it comes from your own harness.

control chart: A statistical process control graph that separates ordinary variation from a real change, so you act only when something has genuinely shifted. Walter Shewhart, Bell Labs, 1920s. Source

cost per completed task: The FinOps Foundation's recommended economic unit for agentic workloads, because a single user action can trigger many underlying model calls, none of them visible in a list price. FinOps Foundation, 2026. Source

cybernetics: The science of control and communication in systems, biological or artificial. It studies how a system holds course toward a goal through anticipation and feedback. Norbert Wiener, 1948. Source

dark matter of identity: The set of accounts invisible to governance but active in the infrastructure, neither reviewed nor decommissioned because no process ever looked for them. Agentic processes fall natively into this category. Coined in identity-security reporting. Source

Deming, W. Edwards: American statistician who took Shewhart's cycle to Japan in the 1950s and turned it into the foundation of modern quality management. 1900 to 1993. Source

DMAIC: Define, measure, analyse, improve, control. The structured cycle for running Six Sigma projects, and the closest methodological relative of MEDIR. Motorola, 1980s; consolidated by General Electric in the 1990s.

drift: Gradual degradation that does not happen in any single run, but across dozens of them. Only sensors running on a cadence detect it.

execution receipt: A compact record of what produced a result: the contract used, the sources consulted, the sensors triggered, the attempts, the cost, who approved it and the rollback point. Extended in part 3 with the rule-of-two answers and the reversal point, for actions with an external effect. Introduced in part 2, extended in part 3.

gate: A point in the flow where nothing moves forward unless a condition is met. Unlike an instruction, a gate cannot be ignored by whoever is executing.

gemba: The real place where work happens. Going to the gemba means observing the process directly instead of discussing the report about it. With agents, it means reading the execution trace, not just the output. Japanese term adopted by lean manufacturing. Source

generative adversarial networks: An architecture in which one network generates and another evaluates, both improving through the contest. It inspired the separation between generator and evaluator agents in long-running harnesses. Ian Goodfellow and colleagues, 2014.

guide: A control that acts before execution, raising the odds the agent gets it right on the first attempt: rules, conventions, examples, the task contract. Called an anticipatory control. Birgitta Böckeler's formulation. Source

hard verification: Deterministic, fast verification that always gives the same answer for the same input. The opposite of judgement verification, which is semantic, expensive and probabilistic.

harness: Everything in an agent except the model: the tools it can use, the context it sees, the memory that survives between sessions, the permissions that bound it, the sensors that measure it and the record of what it did. The word denotes the equipment that channels an animal's strength in a useful direction. Established as a discipline in February 2026. Source

Heinrich, Herbert William: American industrial safety researcher whose 1931 accident triangle proposed that a fixed ratio of near misses precedes each serious injury. The exact ratio is now questioned, but the underlying logic, that a loss event is almost always preceded by a warning, still grounds treating an agent's near misses as a leading indicator. 1886 to 1962. Source

jidoka: Autonomation, or automation with a human touch. The machine detects the abnormality, stops itself and calls for help, so the defect does not travel down the line. Toyota Production System. Source

kaizen: Continuous improvement in small increments, by everyone and all the time, rather than in episodic projects. It is the Reinforce step of the MEDIR cycle. Japanese term spread through the Toyota Production System. Source

kanban: A visual signalling system that authorises production or movement only when real demand exists, preventing accumulation. Taiichi Ohno, Toyota Production System. Source

language model: The reasoning engine. It proposes the next action, but executes nothing on its own: the tools the harness exposes do the executing.

lethal trifecta: Any agent combining access to private data, exposure to untrusted content and the ability to communicate externally can be turned into an exfiltration tool by a single injected instruction. Simon Willison's formulation. Source

life cycle (agent): The states an agent passes through once, from a versioned briefing to decommissioning: briefing, certified, in operation, under review, suspended, decommissioned. Distinct from MEDIR, which repeats inside a single task. Introduced in part 4.

linter: A program that analyses code automatically and flags rule violations before it moves on. With agents, the linter's error message acts as a sensor: when it teaches the fix, the agent repairs itself.

MEDIR: The five-step harness engineering cycle: Map, Equip, Delegate, Inspect and Reinforce. Described in part 1.

mutation testing: A technique that introduces small, deliberate changes into the work and checks whether the tests catch them. It reveals tests that run the code without verifying anything.

non-accumulation rule: The rule that the four agent-governance roles, owner, certifier, auditor and area sponsor, cannot combine in the same person. The organisational counterpart of part 3's separation of powers. Introduced in part 4.

non-human identity: Any identity that does not belong to a person: service accounts, API keys, certificates, automation bots, and now agents. Historically excluded from the identity governance applied to human staff. Introduced in part 3.

on behalf of: A delegation pattern where the agent authenticates using a present human's session and inherits that person's permissions for the duration of the task. The copilot pattern. Introduced in part 3.

OpenTelemetry semantic conventions for generative AI: An open, vendor-neutral vocabulary of standard attribute names for agent telemetry, covering the model called, token counts, finish reason and, where enabled, the content of instructions, tool calls and responses. OpenTelemetry project. Source

OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications: A peer-reviewed security framework for agentic applications, published December 2025 with contributions from over a hundred specialists. OWASP. Source

PDCA: Plan, do, check, act. The iterative cycle of continuous improvement, also known as the Shewhart cycle. A direct ancestor of MEDIR. Walter Shewhart, 1930s. Source

poka-yoke: Mistake-proofing. Redesigning the process so the error becomes impossible, instead of asking whoever executes for more attention. Shigeo Shingo, Toyota Production System. Source

progressive disclosure: The principle by which the agent loads only the information the task requires, in tiers: first the summary, then the body, then the attachments. It is what allows large guides at no reading cost. Anthropic, Agent Skills. Source

prompt: The written instruction given to the model on a run. Important, but only one component of the system, and the only one most people try to adjust when something fails.

pull request: A proposed change to a system, submitted for review before being merged. It is the natural point for a human gate in agent workflows.

regression testing: Verification that detects when a change broke something that used to work. It is the main maintenance sensor for any system that keeps evolving.

retry ceiling: The maximum number of times an agent may repeat a task before stopping and escalating. Without it, a correction loop can burn time and cost indefinitely.

reversal point: The state of the world recorded immediately before an irreversible action executes, logged before execution rather than after, because it is the only version of the record a rollback can actually use. Introduced in part 3.

reversibility: The criterion that organises delegation: a reversible action costs the price of undoing it, an irreversible action costs the price of the mistake. The right yardstick for setting authority limits, not how important the action looks.

rule of two: Three questions about a proposed action, private data access, untrusted content, external communication. Without a human in the loop, an agent may satisfy at most two of them. Attributed to Meta, formulation confirmed by two independent secondary sources, primary publication not located.

sandbox: An isolated environment where execution happens without touching real systems. It allows autonomy to be delegated without a mistake having an external consequence.

sensor: A control that acts after execution, measuring what was produced and handing the agent the information it needs to correct itself. Called a feedback control. Birgitta Böckeler's formulation. Source

separation of powers: The architecture where proposal, authorisation, execution and record keeping are held by four distinct functions that cannot collapse into one. The technical equivalent of segregation of duties in internal control. Introduced in part 3.

Shewhart, Walter: Physicist and statistician at Bell Labs, creator of statistical process control and the control chart, and author of the cycle that became PDCA. 1891 to 1967. Source

skill: A folder containing a SKILL.md file with a name and description header, plus instructions, supporting files and optional code. An open format for packaging procedural knowledge for agents. Anthropic, October 2025; open standard since December 2025. Source

static analysis: Automated verification carried out on the work without running it, looking for rule violations, excessive size, duplication and irregular structure.

task contract: A written definition, made before execution, of what the task delivers, what its limits are and how to know it is done. It is the Map step of the MEDIR cycle.

test coverage: The percentage of the work that was run by tests. It tells you something was executed, not that the effect was verified, which makes it a misleading indicator when used alone.

Three Lines Model: The Institute of Internal Auditors' framework separating management that owns risk (first line), functions that monitor and challenge it (second line), and independent assurance reporting to the governing body (third line). Adopted 2013, revised 2020 and 2023, and the direct precedent for part 4's four roles. Institute of Internal Auditors. Source

Toyota Production System: A production system built on two pillars, just-in-time and jidoka, and the origin of most of the quality vocabulary cited in this series. Taiichi Ohno and Eiji Toyoda, from the 1950s. Source

trigger-type classification: Copilot, human-initiated or ambient. Describes how much human presence exists at the moment an agent acts, mapped against the N0 to N3 autonomy tiers. Introduced in part 3.

vibe coding: A way of programming in which you accept whatever the AI generates without reviewing it, feeding errors back for it to fix. Karpathy, who coined the term in 2025, described it as suited to throwaway weekend projects. Andrej Karpathy, 2025.
